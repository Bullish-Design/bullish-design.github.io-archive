The Eames molded-plywood chairs show how a manufacturing process can shape both a product's form and its assembly. Charles and Ray Eames explored bent plywood in the early 1940s. During World War II, the United States Navy commissioned them to develop molded-plywood splints, stretchers, and glider shells. [Herman Miller's design history](https://www.hermanmiller.com/products/seating/side-chairs/eames-molded-plywood-chairs/design-story/) describes how that work advanced their molding process.

## Let the material set the boundary

After the war, the Eameses applied the process to chairs. Their first idea used one continuous shell, but the plywood did not withstand stress where the seat met the back. They changed to separate molded panels. The chair's shape and joint followed the material's limits.

The Museum of Modern Art records the low side chair (LCM) as a 1946 design. Its molded-plywood seat and back sit on a chrome-plated steel frame, with rubber shock mounts between the shell and frame. The separate panels simplified production, according to [MoMA's object record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/2298).

## A visible joint carries the decision

The chair does not hide the split between seat and back. The joint makes the product architecture legible. The rubber mounts connect the formed shell to the metal frame, while the frame carries the load to the floor.

This is a useful design pattern: test the material, identify where it fails, and let the assembly reflect that limit. A material constraint can lead to a clear product structure when the designer treats it as part of the brief.
