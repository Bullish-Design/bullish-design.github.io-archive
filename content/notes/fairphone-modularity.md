Repairability depends on more than a replaceable battery. A product also needs a clear access path, suitable fasteners, available parts, and instructions. Fairphone's Gen. 6 provides a recent example of these choices working together.

## Plan for service access

Fairphone's July 2025 product announcement says the Gen. 6 has twelve replaceable parts and can be opened with one screwdriver. The listed parts include the battery, screen, camera, and USB port. Its [repair guide](https://www.fairphone.com/repairs) shows the replacement steps for these assemblies.

That design makes repair access part of the physical product. The enclosure, fasteners, connectors, and part boundaries determine which repairs a user can perform. Documentation and spare-part supply complete the service path.

## Choose the size of a replaceable unit

A module groups parts behind one replacement boundary. Larger modules can simplify access, but they can require users to replace more material when one component fails. Smaller modules can isolate a fault, but they add interfaces and assembly decisions.

Fairphone's announcement describes separately replaceable parts, while its repair guides define the practical steps. These sources show a design strategy. They do not measure the lifetime impact of every repair or compare the device with other phones.

## Treat repair as product behavior

When designers specify a replaceable part, they also define how a person reaches it, disconnects it, and returns the product to service. That turns repair into a product behavior with a designed sequence.

The useful lesson is to set the service goal early. Decide which failures the owner can address, what tools each repair needs, and which components should remain separate. Then test those choices against the finished enclosure and instructions.
